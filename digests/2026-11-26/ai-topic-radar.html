Sudoku is a classic example. It’s simple to check the solution (just count the numbers in each row, column, and box), but finding that solution can take a long time. Many ambitious Claude projects have relied on this gap: formalizing proofs in mathematics or migrating large, well-tested codebases are tasks Claude can take on as long as it has a reliable way to verify its work. But as Claude has become more capable, it’s begun to take on projects in live systems, where generating the work is easier than verifying it. In these systems, users often rely on edge cases and quirks that fall outside the test suite , and it’s hard to write a definition of “correct” that covers everything . Without a reliable way to check Claude’s work, the migration of a live system is hard to trust—however quickly Claude can generate the code for it. Related articles Engineering Nov 26, 2025 Effective harnesses for long-running agents View more Many problems have a generator–verifier gap: it’s often far easier to verify that a solution is correct than to come up with one. Sudoku is a classic example. It’s simple to check the solution (just count the numbers in each row, column, and box), but finding that solution can take a long time. Many ambitious Claude projects have relied on this gap: formalizing proofs in mathematics or migrating large, well-tested codebases are tasks Claude can take on as long as it has a reliable way to verify its work. But as Claude has become more capable, it’s begun to take on projects in live systems, where generating the work is easier than verifying it. In these systems, users often rely on edge cases and quirks that fall outside the test suite , and it’s hard to write a definition of “correct” that covers everything . Without a reliable way to check Claude’s work, the migration of a live system is hard to trust—however quickly Claude can generate the code for it.
