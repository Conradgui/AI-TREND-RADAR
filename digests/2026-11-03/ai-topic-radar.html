Towards a Trustworthy and Governable Internet of Agentics: An Architectural Perspective on Protocols, Systems, and Services
ArXiv · 政策监管、社会影响与 AI 安全
59 观察

Large language model applications are increasingly being organized as tool-using and multi-agent workflows rather than as isolated chat interfaces. This perspective paper examines the architectural problem that follows from that shift: agents may reason, invoke tools, and delegate work, but dependable deployment still requires interoperability across frameworks, runtimes, data boundaries, and organizational control domains. We study this problem through the lens of the \emph{Internet of Agentics} (IoA), an emerging service-computing view in which heterogeneous agents are treated as governable service participants rather than only as stand-alone model endpoints. The paper organizes the landscape as a three-layer progression: from today's fragmented agent ecosystem, to interoperable agents, and ultimately to trustworthy and governable autonomy. First, it examines the protocol stack centered on the Agentic AI Foundation, including MCP for agent--tool interaction; A2A for agent--agent coordination; AGENTS.md and Skills-style procedural manifests; and discovery, transport, and trust mechanisms such as AGNTCY and SLIM. Second, it reviews the systems abstractions that make agents deployable as service components, including logical agent architectures, orchestration patterns, gateways, registries, runtime control loops, and hardware-aware inference infrastructure. Third, it situates these developments within services computing, arguing that agentic systems reinterpret classical concerns from SOA and microservices---discovery, composition, governance, pricing, and trust---at the level of cognitive capabilities rather than deterministic functions.

Large language model applications are increasingly being organized as tool-using and multi-agent workflows rather than as isolated chat interfaces. This perspective paper examines the architectural problem that follows from that shift: agents may reason, invoke tools, and delegate work, but dependable deployment still requires interoperability across frameworks, runtimes, data boundaries, and organizational control domains. We study this problem through the lens of the \emph{Internet of Agentics} (IoA), an emerging service-computing view in which heterogeneous agents are treated as governable service participants rather than only as stand-alone model endpoints. The paper organizes the landscape as a three-layer progression: from today's fragmented agent ecosystem, to interoperable agents, and ultimately to trustworthy and governable autonomy. First, it examines the protocol stack centered on the Agentic AI Foundation, including MCP for agent--tool interaction; A2A for agent--agent coordination; AGENTS.md and Skills-style procedural manifests; and discovery, transport, and trust mechanisms such as AGNTCY and SLIM. Second, it reviews the systems abstractions that make agents deployable as service components, including logical agent architectures, orchestration patterns, gateways, registries, runtime control loops, and hardware-aware inference infrastructure. Third, it situates these developments within services computing, arguing that agentic systems reinterpret classical concerns from SOA and microservices---discovery, composition, governance, pricing, and trust---at the level of cognitive capabilities rather than deterministic functions.

Two machines can each recognize an object well and still fail together. We study classifiers that see different parts of one object and send short messages to a receiver that identifies its class. Coordination asks how to design messages together; cooperation asks whether the team outperforms one sender given the whole budget. On three digit datasets, messages designed together beat messages designed apart by 18.07 to 22.68 percentage points on average with two-bit messages, with gains in all 24 configurations. A fixed agreement, you report the row and I the column, recovers about half this gain, with only the receiver learned. Senders that draw the same distinctions waste a narrow vocabulary, and senders whose distinctions cross multiply it. A team has no more possible messages than one sender with the same budget, so a partner must bring evidence the first lacks. A solvable model shows individual statistics alone cannot determine whether a partnership helps. On MNIST digits, six shared bits remove 49.54 percent of the strongest tested solo's errors on average.
