Detecting Shadow AI: Unsanctioned LLM Agents on Enterprise Endpoints: A Taxonomy, an Open Detector and a Controlled Evaluation
ArXiv · 政策监管、社会影响与 AI 安全
59 观察

Shadow IT, the use of technology that the IT organisation has not approved, is a long-standing governance problem. We argue that autonomous AI agents can make it grow much faster: agents make building tools nearly free, act with their user's privileges, and leave behind scripts, automations and MCP servers that are themselves unsanctioned. A simple compounding model shows that, under its assumptions, shadow IT grows exponentially once agentic artifacts reproduce faster than the organisation retires them; retiring them starts with detecting them. We then ask whether unsanctioned agents can be detected on Windows endpoints. We propose a taxonomy of nine classes of agents and six layers of endpoint footprint, and argue that rules based only on the structure of processes and connections cannot separate an agent from its behavioural twin, the same program talking to a non-AI service. We present Agent Detector, an open-source Windows detector that anchors behaviour to the identity of the remote service and correlates it with configuration, installation and execution history, using a catalog of 39 tools and 184 LLM endpoint entries; it informs users through a notice they must acknowledge instead of blocking. On a controlled bench of 12 synthetic agents, 3 AI components and 9 benign workloads, repeated 5 times on one workstation, two behavioural rules flagged every behavioural twin, while Agent Detector flagged none and detected every agent in the majority of runs. Its limits are temporal: a connection to a provider behind a shared content-delivery network is attributable only while the DNS cache holds the provider's name, one to five minutes, and an intermittent agent was seen by about one scan in three. A scan costs about 154 ms. The bench is too small to rank detectors; it maps what each kind of evidence can see: unsanctioned agents can be partly detected, and not by behaviour alone.

Clinical predictions using artificial intelligence in neurosurgery and its subspecialties: a systematic review and meta-analysis
ArXiv · 政策监管、社会影响与 AI 安全
57 观察

Artificial intelligence models for clinical prediction are proliferating across neurosurgery, but their aggregate discriminative performance, distribution along the care pathway and methodological quality are unclear. We systematically reviewed and meta-analysed these studies in neurosurgery and its subspecialities along with their risk of bias appraisal. We performed a PRISMA search for peer-reviewed original studies using AI to predict a clinical outcome in neurosurgical populations. Metrics were pooled using random effects modelling. Each study was classified as diagnostic, prognostic or forecasting and was assigned an evidence level. Clinical prediction task sub-stratification analysis was performed. Risk-of-bias was assessed using PROBAST. 1063 studies were included. Across 451 studies reporting AUC, pooled discrimination was 0.86, ranging from 0.84 (spine) to 0.87 (neuro-oncology), with extreme heterogeneity. Performance varied by clinical pathway stage, highest for diagnosis, grading and molecular tasks and lowest for post-operative tasks. Only 51 studies were using prospective data with external validation, whereas 619 rested on internal validation alone or no validation. 89 percent studies were at high risk-of-bias, driven by the analysis domain. AI models report high performance across all the neurosurgical subspecialities, concordant across AUC, accuracy, sensitivity and specificity and robust to risk-of-bias stratification. Performance is highest for imaging-anchored diagnostic tasks and lowest at decision-facing post-operative stage. However, the literature is dominated by optimism-prone, internally-validated and poorly calibrated models with extreme heterogeneity. External validation, calibration analysis and reporting guidelines adherence remain prerequisites for clinical adoption.

Pricing Symbolic Rules by What GNNs Leave Unlearned
ArXiv · 模型与技术突破
59 观察

Symbolic knowledge (motif counts, ring counts, label constraints) is routinely added to graph neural networks (GNNs) and justified by their limited expressivity. We replace this yardstick with an exact one. The largest log-loss reduction a rule $S$ can give a model family is $I(Y;S\midσ)$ given what the family computes; every realised gain splits exactly into this term plus repaid neural debt; and for a trained network with representation $T$ the value is $I(Y;S\mid T)$, the label information training failed to extract. Rule design thus becomes rule discovery: we score candidates on the network's out-of-fold residual against calibrated permutation nulls with family-wise error control. On ZINC, discovery recovers the hidden ring term $\max(0,\ell_{\max}-6)$ of the target in three independent runs ($p_{\mathrm{adj}}=0.001$) and beats expert cycle counts; frozen and applied to five unseen datasets it is never significantly worse than the plain network or the expert rule, and on BBBP it significantly beats both.

Detecting Shadow AI: Unsanctioned LLM Agents on Enterprise Endpoints: A Taxonomy, an Open Detector and a Controlled Evaluation
ArXiv · 政策监管、社会影响与 AI 安全
59 观察

Shadow IT, the use of technology that the IT organisation has not approved, is a long-standing governance problem. We argue that autonomous AI agents can make it grow much faster: agents make building tools nearly free, act with their user's privileges, and leave behind scripts, automations and MCP servers that are themselves unsanctioned. A simple compounding model shows that, under its assumptions, shadow IT grows exponentially once agentic artifacts reproduce faster than the organisation retires them; retiring them starts with detecting them. We then ask whether unsanctioned agents can be detected on Windows endpoints. We propose a taxonomy of nine classes of agents and six layers of endpoint footprint, and argue that rules based only on the structure of processes and connections cannot separate an agent from its behavioural twin, the same program talking to a non-AI service. We present Agent Detector, an open-source Windows detector that anchors behaviour to the identity of the remote service and correlates it with configuration, installation and execution history, using a catalog of 39 tools and 184 LLM endpoint entries; it informs users through a notice they must acknowledge instead of blocking. On a controlled bench of 12 synthetic agents, 3 AI components and 9 benign workloads, repeated 5 times on one workstation, two behavioural rules flagged every behavioural twin, while Agent Detector flagged none and detected every agent in the majority of runs. Its limits are temporal: a connection to a provider behind a shared content-delivery network is attributable only while the DNS cache holds the provider's name, one to five minutes, and an intermittent agent was seen by about one scan in three. A scan costs about 154 ms. The bench is too small to rank detectors; it maps what each kind of evidence can see: unsanctioned agents can be partly detected, and not by behaviour alone.
